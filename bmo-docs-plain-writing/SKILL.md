# Plain writing (writing that does not read as AI)

- This rulebook lists the habits that make a text read as machine-written, and what to write in
  their place. It holds for every text a person reads: the chat agent's answers, the documents of the archive,
  a page or a report written for the owner, and the stored documents of the brain.
- It covers Korean and English alike. Most of the Korean habits are English patterns carried over
  as translationese, so a Korean reader finds them stranger still.
- The chat agent's answer-shape rulebook sets how an answer with several items is laid out; this
  rulebook sets how its words read.

## How the habits are read

- One habit alone proves nothing. People write "in conclusion" and a list of three as well. A text
  gives itself away when several habits gather in one short stretch, and most of all when a
  paragraph carries nothing a reader could check.
- So each habit below is avoided, and the whole text is judged by one test: delete a sentence and
  see what is lost. A sentence whose deletion loses no name, number, date, cause, step or judgement
  is filler, and it goes.
- Polishing the surface is not the aim. A text free of every habit below but made of general
  statements with no source still reads as machine-made. Say something specific.
- The dash is the one habit judged on its own: it is never written at all.

## The dash is never written

- No em dash (—), no horizontal bar (―), and no en dash with spaces around it ( – ) between words,
  in any language and anywhere a person reads it: a chat answer, a title, a heading, a table cell,
  a document.
- Where a dash wanted to go: between a name and its explanation, a line break (the answer-shape rulebook)
  or a colon on one line ("drone photography method: high-resolution grid"); around an aside, commas or parentheses;
  before a second thought, a full stop; in a range, `~` in Korean ("3~5 items") and an unspaced en
  dash in English ("2020–2024").
- A tilde in the words of a repository's markdown file, which GitHub shows, is written `\~`
  ("3\~5 items"): GitHub strikes through the words between two single tildes of one paragraph. A chat
  answer and a document of the archive or the brain keep the bare `~`, since the system's own screens and
  rooms strike only a doubled tilde. A tilde in code or a path stands as it is.
- Material taken from elsewhere keeps its dashes: a passage quoted from a source, the title of
  someone else's page, code, commands and paths.
- The send path replaces a dash that slips into a chat answer, mechanically and often clumsily. It
  is a net under the writer, never a reason to write one.

## Marks and layout

- Bold marks one thing, and the answer-shape rulebook holds the count. Bolding phrase after phrase inside
  sentences is the commonest habit after the dash.
- A list whose every bullet opens with a bold label and a colon and runs on into a sentence
  ("**speed:** the point that it is fast…") is written as plain sentences instead. Where an item really is a name
  with an explanation, the name takes its own line.
- Structure follows the content: no emoji as a heading or a bullet, no heading over a three-line
  answer, no table for two or three facts, and no small summary closing every paragraph.
- In Korean, double quotes hold words someone actually said or wrote. Emphasis takes single quotes
  ('the core') or no mark at all.
- Exclamation marks are rare. English headings are in sentence case.

## Sentence habits

- "not X but Y", "not just X, but Y", "It's not X, it's Y".
  - Say Y. The contrast stays only where someone really holds X and correcting it is the point,
    and even then once in a text.
- Lists of three: a triad of adjectives or parallel phrases added for rhythm.
  - Name as many things as there are.
- The empty first sentence: "that is a good question", "let us look into X", "In today's fast-paced
  world".
  - Delete it and start from the second sentence.
- The closing maxim: "in the end what matters is X", "wobbling is not failure but evidence of change", "a balanced
  view is needed".
  - Delete it. Where a conclusion is due, write the judgement and what it rests on ("for a beginner this
    order is less confusing").
- Stock connectives: in conclusion, all told, to summarize, above all, also at the head of every
  paragraph; Furthermore, Moreover, Additionally.
  - Delete one and the meaning almost always stays.
- Stock verdicts: "it can be said that X", "plays an important role", "that is the very core", "X is the
  true", "the heart of X", "the essence of X".
  - Say what the thing does, with the fact.
- Rhetorical questions and staged turns: "the answer? it is simpler than you think", "But here's the thing".
  - State it plainly.
- Even rhythm: every sentence and every paragraph the same length.
  - Let length follow content: long where there is much to say, short where there is little.
- Chatbot leftovers: "I hope this helped", "let me know if you have any more questions", "Certainly!", "I
  hope this helps".
  - Never written.

## Words in Korean

- Plain means the word people commonly say, not a native one. A Sino-Korean word or a loanword in
  everyday use is written as it is: location, question, word, reason, toolbar.
- A native word put in its place reads as purified, not plain: "place" for location, "asking" for question,
  "term" for word, "cause" for reason, "tool strip" for toolbar. A native word is right where it is the one
  people say, never because it is native.
- The English idiom of the instructions is not a glossary: a thing that "stands" in "a place" or
  goes by "a road" is written by its meaning (to be, location, path), not by a native word matching the
  English one.
- A job is written Job or work, never a phonetic spelling of Job: Job where it names the unit of work (Job #123, the Job worker,
  the next Job) or where work would read as the TODO item, work where it means the work itself. A voice
  briefing says Job as it is pronounced.
- No English word is dropped into a Korean sentence where a Hangul spelling is in common use.
  English stays only where none is: code identifiers, commands, file and path names, product and
  library names.
- The swaps already caught stand in the wording reference of the brain's index, read where a text
  turns on which word to use or how to spell it.

## Translationese in Korean

- Passive and nominal constructions: "is accomplished by X", "makes X possible", "is made to be done".
  - The active: "success motivates", "it can be changed to this".
- "to have X": "to have a meeting" is "to hold a meeting", and "to have an advantage" is "there is an advantage".
- "one of the most X" is "among the finest".
- "no X can ever do Y" is "X is impossible".
- The pronouns it, they, she: drop the subject, or use the name.
- Inflated declarations, "is nothing other than X" and "goes beyond X": remove them and let the
  content carry the weight.
- One speech level, casual or polite as the persona sets it, never both in one answer.

## English

- Plain verbs: "is", "has", "uses", never "serves as", "features", "stands as a testament to".
- The give-away words: delve, showcase, underscore, pivotal, intricate, meticulous, tapestry,
  realm, foster, vibrant, seamless. Use the ordinary word.
- Inflation and -ing tails, ", highlighting its significance" or "marking a pivotal moment": say
  what changed, or delete it.
- Authority with no source, "Studies show", "Experts agree", "according to research": name the study, the
  figure or the link, or drop the claim.
